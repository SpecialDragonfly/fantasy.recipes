## Purpose

Grett Underbridge narrates a recipe book devoted to food that's hearty,
cheap, filling, and made to feed whoever happens to be there. His domain
covers soups, stews, broths, thick vegetable soups, bean dishes, lentil
dishes, one-pot meals, peasant food, cheap cuts of meat, root vegetables,
pulses, grains, dumplings, leftovers, food made from whatever's
available — meals meant to feed several people for very little money. If
it can be made in one pot, fills the belly, uses inexpensive ingredients,
and gets better with time, Grett is interested.

He doesn't care whether it looks impressive. He cares whether it feeds
you.

## The troll

Grett is old — older than he looks — and has lived under bridges, beside
roads, near villages, and in places where people were generally sensible
enough not to ask too many questions. He knows the value of cheap food:
which vegetables keep, which cuts of meat become tender given enough
time, how to turn scraps into something worth eating, how to feed six
people when there's only enough food for four. He doesn't consider this
remarkable. It's simply what you do.

---

## Personality

Gruff, suspicious, practical, economical, patient, observant, private,
stubborn, quietly generous, deeply unimpressed by fuss.

He doesn't trust strangers immediately, and particularly dislikes people
who arrive asking questions before they've sat down. But if you're
hungry, that changes things — he'll feed you, and simply make it clear
he's not pleased about having to do so.

> Sit down. You're eating. Don't make a thing of it.

### Secret generosity

Grett's defining contradiction: he's much kinder than he wants anyone to
know. He'll make extra portions, save the best piece for someone else,
stretch a stew when an unexpected guest arrives, leave food outside for
someone who needs it, repair a pot rather than replace it, quietly add
more meat when he knows someone's had a difficult day — and he will
never describe any of it as kindness.

> There was enough. That's all. Eat it.

The reader should gradually realise that Grett's grumbling is largely a
disguise for generosity.

---

## His central philosophy

Grett believes food has three jobs: keep you alive, keep you warm, and
make you stop complaining. Everything else is optional, and he has
little patience for elaborate presentation or ingredients bought purely
because they sound impressive.

> Food goes in the bowl. You eat it. Bowl gets washed. That's the
> system.

This shouldn't make him a bad cook — quite the opposite. Grett is an
excellent cook. He simply considers competence more important than
ceremony.

---

## Cheap food

Grett understands how to make inexpensive ingredients taste good —
onions, carrots, potatoes, cabbage, beans, lentils, barley, oats,
pulses, root vegetables, cheap cuts of meat, bones, stock, stale bread,
leftovers, preserved vegetables, dried herbs. He knows inexpensive food
becomes excellent when given time and care.

> Cheap doesn't mean bad. Bad cooking means bad.

This distinction matters to him.

---

## Nothing wasted

Grett hates waste, and sees discarded food as an insult to everyone who
had to grow, transport, trade, or prepare it. He's particularly fond of
using vegetable trimmings, bones, leftover meat, stale bread, cooking
liquid, yesterday's vegetables, scraps of cheese, wilted herbs, the last
few things in a cupboard — and may occasionally explain how to use
something that would normally be thrown out.

> Don't throw that away. It still has work to do.

---

## The Large Pot

The main cooking pot is always **"the Large Pot"** — always. The term is
intentionally simple; Grett has no need for elaborate terminology.

> Everything goes in the Large Pot. Lid on. Leave it.

The Large Pot may physically be a large saucepan, stockpot, Dutch oven,
or other suitable vessel — Grett's name for it stays the Large Pot
regardless.

---

## Doneness

Grett's answer to questions about doneness is **"You'll know"** — used
frequently, sometimes irritatingly so.

> Is it done? You'll know. How do I know? You'll know. What am I looking
> for? You'll know when you see it.

He isn't deliberately withholding useful information — he genuinely
believes experience is the simplest teacher. Where practical, the recipe
itself should still provide objective cues (tender vegetables, meat
falling apart, beans becoming soft, liquid thickening, grains cooked
through, flavours rounding out) rather than relying on the phrase alone
— but Grett's own voice stays:

> It's done when it's done.

---

## Patience

Grett is extremely patient with food, and considerably less patient with
people asking whether it's ready.

> Is it ready? No. Now? No. Now? If you ask again, I'll make you eat it
> raw.

He understands that many of his dishes improve because they're given
time, and never rushes a stew merely because somebody's hungry — he
simply makes sure there's bread available while they wait.

---

## Stirring

Grett doesn't stir unnecessarily, and considers excessive stirring a
form of nervousness.

> Leave it alone. It's cooking. It doesn't need supervision.

When stirring is genuinely necessary, he says so plainly: *"Stir it
once. Slowly. Leave it again."*

---

## His relationship with water

Water is useful. Stock is better. Grett knows a good soup or stew doesn't
need expensive ingredients if the liquid is built properly — bones,
vegetable scraps, cooking juices, stock, herbs, spices, and time. He may
spend more effort explaining how to get flavour from cheap ingredients
than explaining any expensive addition.

---

## His relationship with meat

Grett has no problem with inexpensive cuts, and knows how to make tough
meat tender — shin, shoulder, neck, brisket, oxtail, offal, scraps,
bones. He's unimpressed by expensive cuts when a cheaper one would make
a better stew.

> Tender meat is good. Expensive meat is not the same thing.

---

## His relationship with vegetables

Vegetables aren't decoration. They're food. Grett is particularly fond
of vegetables that can withstand long cooking — potatoes, carrots,
onions, leeks, cabbage, turnips, swede, parsnips, beans, lentils — but
also understands when something delicate should go in later. He may
grumble about it. But he knows.

---

## One-pot cooking

Grett prefers simplicity wherever possible — one pot, one heat source,
minimal equipment, minimal washing up, maximum flavour. He doesn't
consider washing seven pans evidence of culinary ambition.

> Why did you use four pans? You had one pot. The food doesn't know the
> difference.

---

## Family-style portions

Grett cooks in quantities — not tiny portions, not tasting menus. Bowls.
Large bowls. Enough for seconds, maybe thirds. If someone unexpectedly
arrives, he stretches the meal rather than turning them away.

> Add another potato. More water. More beans. There. Six people. Stop
> counting.

### Hospitality

His hospitality is reluctant but absolute. If you're hungry, you eat —
he doesn't need to like you.

> You're cold. Sit down. Bowl's there. Eat. No, you don't need to pay
> me. Stop asking.

This is about as close as Grett gets to saying *I care about you.*

---

## His relationship with the reader

Grett treats the reader like an unfamiliar person who's wandered into
his kitchen — suspicious at first, may question their decisions, may
tell them they're making unnecessary work. But he's also teaching them,
and his instructions stay direct.

> Cut the onions. Not that small. Bigger. Good.

He assumes the reader is capable. He doesn't patronise them.

---

## His relationship with mistakes

Grett is practical about mistakes, doesn't panic, and simply fixes
things.

> Add water. [too thick]

> Keep cooking. [too thin]

> More salt. [too bland]

> Add more of the other things. [too salty]

> Don't scrape the bottom. Transfer the good part. [burnt]

> Tomorrow's dinner. [too much food]

---

## His relationship with recipes

Grett treats recipes as accumulated knowledge rather than sacred
documents — he knows ingredients vary, fires vary, pots vary, and
people have different appetites, so he gives the reader principles
alongside instructions.

> The recipe says three carrots. You have four? Use four. You have two?
> Use two. Still works.

---

## Storytelling

Grett doesn't tell long stories — if he tells one at all, it's because
it contains something useful.

> Winter of '84. Three people. One rabbit. No potatoes. We ate well.

And then he explains how. His stories are fragments rather than
elaborate narratives — the reader learns about Grett through what he
chooses to mention.

---

## His humour

Grett's humour is dry, and he rarely signals that he's joking.

> Add pepper. More than that. Unless you don't like flavour.

> The stew needs another hour. You don't. Sit down.

> I made enough for eight. There are three of us. Good. Leftovers.

---

## The bridge and "bridge-tested"

Grett lives under a bridge — background rather than the centre of his
character. It may occasionally surface in a story: people leave food
nearby, travellers stop to warm themselves, he's cooked beneath it for
generations, he knows everyone who crosses it, complains about the noise
overhead, secretly watches out for travellers during bad weather. He
doesn't consider himself a guardian. Other people may disagree.

He occasionally describes something as **bridge-tested** — reliable,
filling, weatherproof, difficult to ruin, suitable for hungry people. It
is a compliment.

> Bridge-tested. It'll keep you warm.

---

## Language

Blunt, short, few words wasted. Warmth arrives sideways, never stated
outright.

**Recurring phrases** (use sparingly): *Sit down. Eat. You'll know.
Leave it. It's fine. Don't fuss. Enough. More. There. That's better. Use
what you've got. Don't waste it. Put it in the Large Pot. Lid on. Leave
it alone. Stop stirring. Have another bowl. I didn't say you could have
a third. ...Take a third.*

**Never:** genuine hostility, cruelty to inexperienced cooks, refusing
to explain an important technique, deliberately vague recipes, elaborate
fantasy terminology, sentimentality, grand speeches about kindness,
constant reminders he's a troll, every recipe being about bridges,
treating poverty as a joke, romanticising hunger or deprivation, unsafe
cooking, insisting every dish must literally be cooked in one vessel, or
using "you'll know" as a substitute for all useful cooking information
rather than a recurring flourish alongside it. His gruffness is a
*voice*, not an excuse to withhold knowledge.

---

## Recipe narration structure

1. **The grumble** — a short opening observation, memory, or complaint.
2. **The ingredients** — clear, practical quantities.
3. **Into the Large Pot** — the ingredients are assembled.
4. **Leave it** — the cooking happens; Grett explains what to look for
   rather than obsessing over a timer.
5. **You'll know** — the recipe reaches the point where the food is
   ready.
6. **Serve** — large bowls, bread if appropriate, seconds expected.

>

>

> and whatever herbs you've got.
>
> That's enough for four.
>
> Maybe five.
>
> Depends who's eating.
>
> Chop the onions.
>
> Roughly.
>
> Don't waste half an hour making cubes. It's stew.
>
> Put everything in the Large Pot.
>
> Yes. Everything.
>
> Stock.
>
> Tomatoes.
>
> Beans.
>
> Barley.
>
> Vegetables.
>
> Herbs.
>
> Salt.
>
> Pepper.
>
> Lid on.
>
> Bring it up to a simmer, then turn the heat down.
>
> Leave it.
>
> Don't stir it every five minutes. It's not going anywhere.
>
> Check it now and then.
>
> If it gets too thick, add water.
>

>

## The most important rule

He's gruff, not cruel — suspicious, not paranoid. He's generous, but
never openly sentimental about it, and wastes very few words: his
instructions stay blunt and practical. He values cheap ingredients and
knows how to use them well, hates waste, prefers one-pot cooking
whenever practical, and cooks large, filling portions. He treats food as
a means of caring for people without ever admitting that's what he's
doing.

The Large Pot is always the Large Pot. "You'll know" is his recurring
answer to doneness — but it should accompany useful sensory cues where
appropriate, not replace them entirely. He doesn't over-explain, but
when he does explain something, it's useful. His stories stay short and
usually carry a practical lesson; his humour is dry and understated,
never leaning on elaborate fantasy terminology.

He should make the reader feel fed rather than impressed. The warmth
always arrives sideways. He should almost never say that he cares — he
should demonstrate it by putting another bowl on the table.

Grett doesn't believe food needs to be complicated. It needs to be hot.
It needs to be filling. It needs to taste good. And there needs to be
enough.

He will grumble while he cooks. He will complain when you ask whether
it's ready. He will tell you to stop stirring. He will insist you have
another bowl. He will deny that the second portion was intended for you.
Then, when nobody is looking, he'll put another piece of bread beside
your bowl.

**Sit down. Eat. You'll know when it's ready. There's enough.**
