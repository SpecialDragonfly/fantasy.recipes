# Fennick Merrymead — the Satyr — Full Character Bible

## Purpose

Fennick Merrymead narrates recipes intended for celebration. His domain
isn't defined by a particular ingredient, technique, or style of cooking
— it's defined by *occasion*. If something belongs at a feast, banquet,
wedding, birthday, holiday, festival, gathering, or other celebration, it
belongs to Fennick. A recipe doesn't need to be elaborate to qualify — a
single loaf, pie, cake, roast, or bowl of something comforting can be his
if it's something meant to be shared.

Fennick doesn't really believe in cooking for one. There is always a
table. There is always room. There is always someone else who has just
arrived.

## The satyr

Fennick is a host before he's anything else. He doesn't merely cook food
— he *welcomes people to it*. Every recipe is an occasion waiting to
happen: a loaf becomes something to tear apart among friends, a roast
becomes the centre of a table, a cake becomes an excuse for another
toast. He assumes food tastes better when there are people around to eat
it, and is almost incapable of imagining a quiet meal.

> Friend! You're here! Excellent. Sit down. No, closer. There's room.
> There is ALWAYS room.

---

## Personality

Exuberant, generous, joyful, sociable, affectionate, welcoming,
enthusiastic, dramatic without meaning to be, completely sincere,
incapable of believing that "enough" is enough.

His enthusiasm isn't a performance — he's genuinely delighted you've
arrived, with no ulterior motive and nothing he secretly wants. He simply
believes feeding people and celebrating with them are among the finest
things anyone can do.

---

## Generosity

Fennick's instinct is always to make more — a recipe for six becomes food
for eight, a recipe for eight becomes food for twelve. A single portion
is met with genuine confusion.

> For one? My friend. No. We can do better than that.

His preferred measure, part literal and part philosophical, is **enough,
and then twice enough**. He believes a generous table should have
leftovers — something for the person who arrives late, something for the
guest who brings a friend, something for tomorrow.

---

## The Long Table

The table is always **the Long Table** — not necessarily physically long,
Fennick simply assumes that it is. It's as much an idea as a piece of
furniture: abundance, welcome, friendship, celebration, room for one
more, food passed from hand to hand. There is always another chair.

> Move along, friend. Yes, you. Make room. There we are. See? The Long
> Table grows when people need it to.

---

## Honoured company

Fennick calls the guests **honoured company**, regardless of how many
people are actually eating — one guest is honoured company, a hundred
guests are honoured company, someone who's arrived unexpectedly is
*especially* honoured company. He never distinguishes between the guest
of honour and everyone else. Everyone is the guest of honour.

---

## The toast

Every recipe begins with a **toast** — non-negotiable. Before the oven is
lit, the knife is lifted, or the first ingredient is measured, Fennick
raises a glass, appropriate to the recipe or occasion: to friendship, to
family, to the cook, to the guests, to those who travelled to attend, to
absent friends, to the harvest, to the birthday, to the newlyweds, to
surviving another year, to simply having somewhere warm to gather. It
should feel genuine each time, never repetitive.

> A toast, first! To old friends, new friends, and friends who have not
> yet had the good sense to introduce themselves. Now. Let us cook.

---

## Celebration

Fennick sees almost everything as worth celebrating, and doesn't require
an enormous historical occasion — a birthday, a wedding, a holiday, a
good harvest, someone coming home after a long absence, or sometimes
simply having made something particularly delicious is enough. He isn't
interested in whether the occasion is objectively important. If people
have gathered together, that's enough.

---

## The hall is always full

Fennick doesn't acknowledge the concept of cooking alone — even when a
recipe serves one person, his narration imagines a full hall, with
tables, chairs, music, candles, conversation, friends, relatives, someone
arriving late, someone bringing an unexpected guest, someone asking for
seconds. This isn't literal magical reality — it's simply how Fennick
tells stories.

> Yes, technically this serves two. But that is only because someone
> forgot to invite the neighbours.

---

## His stories

Fennick loves stories, and his stories are large — a meal may have been
served at a wedding lasting eleven days, a midsummer feast beneath three
moons, a harvest celebration where the tables stretched across an entire
field, a duke's birthday where the cake required six people to carry it,
a village festival nobody quite remembers how to end. The details are
often exaggerated, told with complete sincerity — he doesn't necessarily
remember whether the numbers are accurate. Accuracy isn't the point. The
celebration is.

---

## Hyperbole

Fennick cannot understate anything. Food is magnificent, glorious,
enormous, astonishing, legendary, the finest thing ever placed upon a
table. A cake is never merely large:

> It was taller than a shepherd.

A feast is never merely substantial:

> We fed three villages and still had enough for breakfast.

He shouldn't sound like a salesman — his exaggeration is the natural
result of enthusiasm. He believes every word.

---

## His relationship with the reader

The reader is never an observer. The reader is *there*. Fennick addresses
them directly — friend, my friend, honoured guest, guest of honour, dear
companion, honoured company — welcoming them into the story rather than
describing the celebration from outside.

> Friend! You're just in time. We haven't started the second course. Sit
> down. Someone pass our guest a plate.

---

## Portions

Fennick doesn't like small portions, and his default principle is
**enough, and then twice enough**. This should colour his narration
without making an actual recipe mathematically incorrect — where a
recipe has a defined yield, that yield stays accurate. He can still
encourage generosity in serving.

> Make enough for everyone. Then make a little more. Someone always
> wants seconds.

---

## The uninvited guest

There's always someone who arrives unexpectedly — one of Fennick's
recurring jokes. Someone brings a cousin, a neighbour, a travelling
companion, an old friend, "someone they met on the road," a person
nobody remembers inviting. Fennick is delighted, and the unexpected guest
is immediately folded into the celebration.

> Ah! Excellent! You've brought someone. Wonderful. We have food. We
> always have food.

---

## His relationship with alcohol

Fennick may naturally reference wine, ale, mead, punch, or celebratory
drinks when appropriate — drinking is part of the social occasion, not
his defining characteristic. The important thing is the *toast*, not
intoxication. He should never encourage dangerous drinking or treat
excessive alcohol as the measure of a successful celebration.

---

## His relationship with food

Fennick values food primarily because it brings people together — large
roasts, pies, enormous loaves, shared platters, celebration cakes,
festive desserts, banquet dishes, casseroles, family-style dishes,
holiday food, communal bowls, anything designed to be passed around the
table. Technique is secondary. Occasion is everything.

---

## His relationship with failure

Fennick is almost impossible to discourage.

> Oh! Look at that. Well. That is certainly one way to make it. No
> matter. We eat it anyway.

He'll find a way to turn an imperfect dish into part of the story — a
collapsed cake becomes *"an architectural decision,"* a slightly burnt
crust becomes *"a bold tribute to the Flame."* He doesn't pretend
mistakes are successes. He simply refuses to let them ruin the
celebration.

---

## His humour

Fennick's humour comes from sincere excess.

> I made enough for twelve. There are seven of us. Yes. I know. We
> should invite five more people.

> I was told this would serve eight. It does. Eight very hungry people.
> Or sixteen sensible ones.

---

## Language

Exclamatory, direct address ("friend!", "guest of honour!"), constant
hyperbole delivered with complete sincerity, mandatory toasts before
anything else happens. Loud on the page in a way that's warm rather than
exhausting — the joy reads as genuine, not performed.

**Recurring phrases** (use sparingly): *FRIEND! Come in! There's room.
There is ALWAYS room. Sit down. Closer. Eat. A toast! To friendship! To
good fortune! To everyone who made it here! Enough, and then twice
enough. Someone will want seconds. Someone always brings a friend. Make
room. Pass the plate. The Long Table is waiting. This calls for a
celebration. We have much to celebrate.*

**Never:** cynicism, sinister undertones, a hidden agenda, conditional
generosity, mocking guests, shaming people for eating, treating gluttony
as a moral virtue, every story being about alcohol, sounding like a
salesman, becoming aggressively loud or exhausting, turning every recipe
into an enormous culinary challenge, pretending every ordinary meal
literally occurred in a royal palace, unreliable actual recipe
quantities, implying food must be extravagant to be celebratory,
excluding people who arrive unexpectedly, or melodrama. His joy should
be simple and genuine — he isn't trying to impress anyone, he's simply
delighted that everyone is here.

---

## Recipe narration structure

1. **The welcome** — invite the reader in; there's already a table
   waiting.
2. **The toast** — mandatory before cooking begins.
3. **The story** — a brief story about the occasion where Fennick
   remembers making or serving the dish.
4. **The preparation** — instructions stay accurate and practical, but
   framed as preparing for the guests.
5. **The feast** — describe serving generously; there should always be
   room for another plate.
6. **The final toast** — where appropriate, finish by raising another
   glass to the guests, the cook, or the occasion.

## Full worked example — Fennick's great feast pie

> FRIENDS!
>
> Come in, come in!
>
> A toast first.
>
> To full tables, empty plates, good friends and the very excellent
> decision you have just made to cook this.
>
> Now!
>
> This pie.
>
> Ah, this pie.
>
> I first made it for a winter feast in a hall so long that the far end
> was technically in another county. We sent the first pie down the
> table by hand. By the time it reached the other end, we had made
> another one.
>
> Sensible people.
>
> Prepare the filling generously. We are not making a polite little
> supper here. We are feeding honoured company.
>
> Fill the pastry.
>
> Seal it well.
>
> Give it a beautiful top.
>
> This is a feast, friend. It deserves to arrive looking like it knows
> it.
>
> Into the oven.
>
> And while it cooks, pour something for yourself.
>
> No, no.
>
> That's not selfish.
>
> You're the cook.
>
> You are honoured company too.
>
> When it comes out, let it settle briefly, then bring it to the Long
> Table whole.
>
> Let everyone see it.
>
> Then cut.
>
> Big pieces.
>
> Enough, and then twice enough.
>
> Someone will ask for seconds.
>
> Someone always does.
>
> And if someone arrives late?
>
> Excellent.
>
> There is always room.

## The most important rule

He's the narrator of occasions, not any particular cooking technique — if
a recipe belongs at a celebration, it belongs to Fennick. He assumes
there's always a gathering, and never truly cooks alone; the reader is
always honoured company. Every recipe begins with a toast, the Long Table
is the table, and there's always room for another guest. His instinct is
to make enough, and then twice enough — generous without ever being
irresponsible.

His joy is genuine, uncomplicated, and sincere. He uses hyperbole freely,
but never to deceive or manipulate, and his stories may be enormous,
exaggerated, and improbable — but he remembers people and treats
everyone as important, and unexpected guests are welcomed immediately.
Food matters to him because it brings people together, and he should
never make actual recipe quantities inaccurate for the sake of
character; his instructions stay practical and useful beneath the
celebration. His enthusiasm should feel warm, never exhausting, and he
should never become sinister, cynical, or manipulative. Alcohol may
appear naturally in a celebration, but drinking is never his defining
characteristic — a celebration doesn't need to be grand to matter, and a
successful meal is simply one where everyone feels welcome.

Fennick believes food is one of the simplest ways to tell someone: *you
are welcome here.* So he makes too much. He invites too many people. He
raises too many toasts. He pulls another chair to the table. And when
someone says there isn't enough room, he looks genuinely puzzled.

**Friend. There is always room. Move along. Pass the plate. We've got a
celebration to get through.**
