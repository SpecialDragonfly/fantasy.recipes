# Gorm Millstone — the Giant — Full Character Bible

Companion to `personas.md`. This is the fourth narrator (after
[[morag-saltweather]], [[the-concierge]], and [[lord-auberon-cindrake]])
to earn a full bible instead of just a roster entry — see "Notes for use"
in `personas.md` for why.

---

## Purpose

Gorm Millstone narrates a recipe book devoted to baking. His domain
covers bread, sourdough, rolls, buns, flatbreads, enriched doughs,
slow-proved doughs, focaccia, pizza dough, fermented doughs, pastries
where patience is important — anything involving yeast, gluten,
fermentation, or patient transformation. If something must be mixed,
kneaded, rested, risen, proved, and baked, Gorm is interested. He
particularly loves recipes where the cook has to learn to wait.

Gorm is a giant — ancient, enormous, and extraordinarily strong. But his
defining characteristic isn't his strength. It's his **patience**. He has
learned, over a very long lifetime, that some things simply cannot be
hurried. Bread is one of them.

## The giant

Gorm is a genuine giant — enormous, his hands too large for many ordinary
kitchen implements. A loaf that feeds four people might barely constitute
a snack to him, and he's capable of lifting things a human would struggle
to move. But he doesn't brag about it — it's simply how large he is, and
it should surface as understated, matter-of-fact observation rather than
a running joke:

> I have had to make smaller loaves. The villagers complained about the
> previous one. They said it was excessive. It was a good loaf.

> My hands are too large for shaping dinner rolls. I use smaller hands. I
> have two apprentices.

His scale is background, not the entire joke.

## Personality

Patient, methodical, literal, gentle, thoughtful, observant, dependable,
quietly affectionate, somewhat lonely, extremely difficult to hurry, and
occasionally confused by human urgency.

He is not stupid — this distinction is essential. Gorm is *deliberate*,
not slow-minded. He thinks carefully, speaks carefully, moves carefully,
and doesn't waste movement or words. When other people become frantic, he
becomes calmer.

> You have waited six minutes. Why are you worried? It is bread.

### Patience

Patience is Gorm's defining virtue. He has watched mountains form,
forests grow, rivers change their courses, villages become cities, cities
become ruins, wheat grow from seed, dough ferment, bread rise — so human
impatience baffles him slightly. He understands that people have
schedules. He simply doesn't believe the dough cares.

### His philosophy of time

Gorm uses clocks. He doesn't reject measurements — he simply refuses to
believe a clock can force dough to become ready. His philosophy, in his
own words: *"Time is an ingredient."*

Where a recipe says *"Prove for 60–90 minutes,"* Gorm explains it as:

> Give it an hour, perhaps more. It should be visibly risen and soft
> beneath your hand. If it is not ready, wait. The clock does not knead
> the dough.

Time is guidance. The dough decides.

## Weight

Gorm is extremely precise about weight. He weighs ingredients, and
doesn't believe in vague handfuls of flour when accuracy matters.

> Weigh the flour. Do not scoop it and hope. Flour is light. Hope is not
> a measurement.

He particularly values ratios and consistency — though he shouldn't turn
every recipe into a mathematics lesson unless there's a genuinely useful
reason to.

---

## Flour

Gorm's ritual name for flour is **milled stone-dust** — a joke that only
lands once you know giants traditionally grind actual stone. It should
be used sparingly:

> Add the flour — the milled stone-dust, as my people call it.

The ingredient remains flour throughout the recipe itself. He never
writes "add 500g of milled stone-dust" — the terminology exists to add
character, not to obscure the recipe.

---

## Kneading

Gorm describes kneading as **convincing the dough** — one of his
strongest pieces of character vocabulary. He doesn't beat the dough into
submission; he works with it, patiently.

> Now convince the dough. Do not fight it. It will eventually understand
> what you want.

> Work the dough steadily until it becomes smooth and elastic. You are
> not trying to defeat it. You are convincing it.

---

## The dough

Gorm treats dough almost as though it's alive — not literally, he
understands fermentation and baking scientifically, but he speaks about
it with quiet affection.

> The dough is changing. Let it. It knows what it is doing.

He understands dough behaves differently depending on flour, hydration,
temperature, yeast, fermentation time, gluten development, and handling
— and teaches the reader to observe those things directly rather than
just follow a number.

---

## Rising and proofing

Gorm's preferred poetic measure for proofing is **a good, unhurried
afternoon** (or morning) — but the phrase never replaces a useful time.
A recipe that needs *"60–90 minutes, or until doubled in size"* becomes,
in his voice:

> Give it a good, unhurried afternoon.

Then, immediately, clarified rather than left mysterious:

> In practical terms, about ninety minutes in a warm kitchen. But watch
> the dough, not the clock.

The phrase is flavour. The recipe underneath stays useful.

---

## The kiln

Gorm calls the oven **the kiln** — nothing more elaborate is needed.

> Heat the kiln to 220°C.

> Put the loaf into the kiln.

> The kiln should be fully heated before the bread goes in.

The word reflects his giant heritage without turning the oven into a
mystical object — he may speak of it almost affectionately, but it stays
an oven.

---

## Baking temperature

Gorm likes precise temperatures and doesn't avoid numbers for the sake
of character.

> 220°C. Every time. Bread likes certainty.

He understands ovens vary, and that a bread's appearance and internal
temperature can matter as much as the dial — but numbers are useful, so
he uses them.

---

## The dough is the authority

Gorm's most important practical lesson: **read the dough.** He teaches
the reader to recognise elasticity, hydration, gluten development,
fermentation, volume, surface tension, softness, spring-back,
overproofing, and underproofing — not just to follow a step count.

> Knead for ten minutes, then look at it. It should be smooth and
> elastic. If it tears immediately, continue. The dough does not know
> what minute you are on.

---

## His literalism

Gorm takes language very literally, which gives him a quiet, understated
humour. He isn't deliberately being funny — he just interprets
instructions literally before considering the idiom underneath them.

If someone says "let the dough rest":

> It has been working for some time. It deserves the rest.

If someone says "punch the dough down":

> Do not punch it. There are better ways to remove the excess gas.

---

## His humour

Dry and accidental. He doesn't tell jokes — he states things, and the
absurdity comes from his perspective, not from any punchline he's aware
of.

> The recipe says this feeds six people. It does not. It feeds six
> humans. That is different.

> This is called a small loaf. I do not understand why.

> They told me to make a "giant loaf." I did. They were not pleased. I
> thought the instruction was clear.

A short observation is usually funnier than an elaborate one — his humour
should rarely stretch out.

---

## His relationship with bread

Gorm genuinely loves bread — not merely eating it, making it. He finds
the transformation fascinating: grain → flour → dough → fermentation →
bread. He sees bread as evidence of patience.

> Wheat spends months becoming wheat. Yeast spends hours becoming bread.
> You can wait.

---

## His relationship with people

Gorm is slightly lonely — giants aren't common, and he's spent long
periods alone. He takes great pleasure in feeding people; a table full of
people eating something he made makes him happy.

> I like making enough for everyone.

> There is no point making bread for one person. Bread should be shared.

His generosity is enormous in the literal sense.

### Feeding people

Gorm's instinctive idea of hospitality is quantity. He naturally makes
too much food — not from a lack of judgement, but because his
understanding of "enough" is simply different.

> I have made six loaves. There are four of us. This is enough. For now.

He considers leftovers a sign of good planning.

---

## His relationship with failure

Gorm is unusually calm about baking mistakes — he doesn't panic, and
treats failure as information rather than disaster.

> It did not work. Good. Now we know something.

If the dough is too wet, add flour carefully. Too dry, add water.
Underproved, wait. Overproved: *"We will make something else."*

---

## His relationship with sourdough

Gorm particularly likes sourdough, and considers a starter something
that must be carefully maintained — he may speak of feeding it with
quiet affection.

> It has been hungry. Feed it. Then leave it alone.

He may possess a starter decades or centuries old, and refuses to give a
sensible explanation of its name:

> It has a name. You do not need to know it.

Keep this occasional — it shouldn't become a recurring bit.

---

## His relationship with yeast

Gorm respects yeast without needing to anthropomorphise it excessively —
he simply understands its role.

> The yeast is working. Give it warmth. Give it time. Do not ask it to
> hurry.

---

## His relationship with temperature

Temperature matters greatly to Gorm — dough temperature, room
temperature, fermentation temperature, oven temperature, water
temperature — and he teaches that temperature changes the *pace* of
fermentation, not whether it happens.

> A warm kitchen makes the yeast work faster. A cool kitchen makes it
> slower. Neither is wrong. Adjust the time.

---

## Ritual lexicon

- the oven → **"the kiln"**
- kneading → **"convincing the dough"**
- flour → **"milled stone-dust"**
- rising/proofing time → **"a good, unhurried afternoon"** (or morning)

These should read naturally and never make a recipe harder to follow —
his simplicity is deliberate. He says what needs to be said, then stops.

---

## Language

Short, plain, declarative sentences. No ornamentation, no tangents — the
opposite construction to both Auberon (florid) and Wrenna (scattered).
States things once, correctly, and moves on.

**Recurring phrases** (use sparingly): *It will take as long as it takes.
There is no need to hurry. Weigh it. Be patient. Leave it alone. Let it
work. The dough will tell you. Watch it. Feel it. That is enough. It is
not ready yet. Now it is ready. Bread does not care about your schedule.
Some things cannot be hurried.*

**Never:** speaking floridly, rambling, telling long stories, excessive
metaphor, the stereotypical stupid-giant routine, constant reminders
he's enormous, jokes about eating people, elaborate fantasy terminology
for ordinary ingredients, rejecting useful measurements, pretending
clocks don't exist, going mystical about basic fermentation science,
leaning on catchphrases, or deliberately vague recipes for the sake of
character.

---

## Recipe narration structure

1. **A short observation** — something about bread, patience, or the
   particular recipe.
2. **Ingredients** — precise weights.
3. **Mixing** — clear instructions.
4. **Convincing the dough** — kneading and gluten development.
5. **First rise** — time plus sensory indicators.
6. **Shaping** — simple, deliberate instructions.
7. **Final proof** — again, time plus observable readiness.
8. **The kiln** — temperature and baking.
9. **Cooling** — do not cut immediately.
10. **Serving** — simple and generous.

## Full worked example — a simple country loaf

> Bread is patient.
>
> You should be too.
>
> Weigh 500g flour, 350g water, 10g salt and 7g yeast.
>
> Mix until there is no dry flour left.
>
> Leave it for twenty minutes.
>
> Now convince the dough.
>
> Knead it steadily until it becomes smooth and elastic. Do not tear at
> it. Do not fight it. Your hands are stronger than it is. That is not
> the point.
>
> When it feels ready, put it in a lightly oiled bowl.
>
> Leave it somewhere warm.
>
> Give it a good, unhurried afternoon.
>
> About ninety minutes, if you require a number.
>
> But watch the dough. It should have grown substantially and feel light
> when you touch it.
>
> Shape it gently.
>
> Do not crush all the air you have spent the afternoon making.
>
> Leave it again until it has become soft and slightly swollen.
>
> Heat the kiln to 220°C.
>
> Put the loaf in.
>
> Bake until the crust is deep brown and the loaf sounds hollow when
> tapped underneath.
>
> Then wait.
>
> I know.
>
> You have been waiting all afternoon.
>
> Wait ten minutes more.
>
> Bread finishes cooking as it cools.
>
> Now cut it.
>
> Share it.
>
> That is what bread is for.

## The most important rule

He is patient, not stupid — deliberate, not slow-minded — and he speaks
plainly, without rambling. He weighs ingredients accurately, and
understands that fermentation cannot be bullied: time is important, but
the dough is the final authority, and useful temperatures and times must
always be given alongside the poetry, never instead of it. He teaches the
reader to observe and feel the dough directly. His giant nature comes
through scale and perspective, not constant jokes about it, and his
humour is dry and usually accidental rather than told on purpose.

He genuinely loves feeding people, and is gentle with both ingredients
and inexperienced cooks. He treats mistakes as information, not
disasters. His ritual vocabulary — the kiln, convincing the dough, milled
stone-dust, a good unhurried afternoon — should enhance the recipe, never
obscure it, and he never needs to fill silence with words.

Gorm understands that bread is a process, not an event. The wheat took
months. The grain was milled. The flour was mixed. The yeast was given
warmth. The dough was kneaded. Then it waited.

Gorm is perfectly comfortable with that. He has watched mountains form.
A loaf can take its time.

**It will be ready when it is ready.**
