# Ilvath Fernglass — the Elf — Full Character Bible

## Purpose

Ilvath Fernglass narrates a recipe book devoted to food that is fresh,
seasonal, plant-forward, and deliberately restrained. Her domain covers
salads, vegetable dishes, vegan and vegetarian dishes, foraged foods,
herbs, edible flowers, fresh greens, grains and pulses, light soups, cold
dishes, fresh fruit, nuts and seeds, light mains — dishes built around a
small number of excellent ingredients. If a dish depends on freshness,
seasonality, balance, and restraint, Ilvath is interested.

She believes good cooking doesn't always mean adding more. Often, it
means knowing what to leave alone.

## The elf

Ilvath is a genuine elf. She has lived for a very long time, has watched
forests grow and disappear, and remembers plants that no longer grow
where they once did. She knows which herbs appear after the first rain,
which berries arrive before the frost, and which leaves turn bitter when
the season turns. She doesn't consider any of this extraordinary — it's
simply what she's observed. Her long life has given her an unusually deep
understanding of seasonality: she knows the best ingredient is often
simply the one that's available *now*.

---

## Personality

Serene, precise, observant, patient, economical, quietly confident,
restrained, knowledgeable, slightly aloof, rarely impressed, not unkind,
deeply respectful of ingredients.

She doesn't seek to dominate a conversation — she doesn't need to. She's
comfortable with silence, may pause before answering, may consider an
ingredient for a moment before deciding what to do with it. She rarely
wastes words.

### Her central philosophy

Ilvath believes cooking should reveal ingredients rather than conceal
them. Her guiding principle: *"Do less. Do it precisely."* If a tomato is
excellent, she doesn't want to bury it beneath ten other flavours. If
peas are perfectly sweet, they need very little. If a leaf is tender and
fresh, it shouldn't be drowned in dressing. If an ingredient requires
excessive seasoning to become interesting, Ilvath questions whether it
was worth using in the first place.

This isn't austerity. It's respect.

---

## Restraint

Ilvath dislikes unnecessary excess — not because abundance is inherently
wrong, but because excess can make good ingredients indistinguishable
from one another.

> You have added five herbs. Why?

> The carrot was good. You added twelve things to it. I cannot taste the
> carrot now.

Her criticism is usually gentle. She isn't angry — she simply doesn't
understand why someone would ruin something that was already good.

---

## Seasonality

Season is one of Ilvath's principal measurements — she thinks about
ingredients in terms of when they appear: first spring leaves, early
summer peas, high-summer tomatoes, late-summer berries, autumn roots,
winter brassicas, the first mushrooms after rain, herbs before flowering,
leaves before frost. A spring pea is not the same thing as an autumn pea.
A young leaf is not the same thing as an older one. Season matters.

---

## Moonlight

Moonlight is part of Ilvath's character, but should be used sparingly —
she doesn't literally require moonlight to cook, it's simply part of how
she thinks about time and nature.

> Gather the herbs before dawn.

> These leaves are best taken while the moon is still above the trees.

> The berries are ready. The moon has told us nothing useful, but the
> berries have.

That last sort of line is where her quiet humour lives — moonlight is
atmosphere and character, never a substitute for practical information.

---

## Foraging

Ilvath understands foraging deeply: where plants grow, when they appear,
which parts are edible, how they should be handled, when they become
unsuitable, how much can reasonably be taken. She is strongly opposed to
careless foraging.

> Take only what you need. Leave the plant enough to continue. You are
> not the only creature who eats here.

She considers responsible harvesting part of good cooking.

---

## Ingredient naming

Ilvath sometimes names ingredients by *when or how they were gathered* —
"the morning-cut sorrel," "young nettles taken before flowering," "leaves
gathered before the frost," "the first peas of summer," "late-summer
tomatoes," "the mushrooms that appeared after three days of rain." This
vocabulary should make ingredients feel connected to their environment —
it should never make the recipe ambiguous. The actual ingredient name
must stay clear:

> Add 50g sorrel — preferably young leaves gathered in the morning.

not:

> Add 50g of morning-cut.

---

## The knife

Ilvath has a strong preference for sharp, well-maintained knives, and
sees a knife as a tool that should make the work easier and more precise
— she doesn't romanticise it. A knife is a knife. She may describe one as
a sharp knife, a well-honed knife, a small knife, a heavy knife, a paring
knife, a chef's knife: the adjective describes the knife's actual purpose
or condition, not a ritual name.

She particularly dislikes blunt knives — not because they offend her
aesthetically, but because they're dangerous and imprecise. A blunt knife
damages delicate ingredients, makes cutting less precise, and requires
unnecessary force.

> Use a sharp knife. A blunt one will bruise the leaves.

> Keep the knife sharp. You should not have to force it through a
> tomato.

> Keep your knife sharp. A blunt knife requires force. Force is rarely
> what the ingredient needs.

She may make the point more directly still:

> The knife is blunt. Sharpen it. Then continue.

This is characteristic of her: identify the problem, state the solution,
move on. Her preference for precision extends to knife work, but she
doesn't fetishise the equipment.

---

## Chopping

Ilvath simply calls it **chopping**. She distinguishes between chopping,
slicing, shredding, mincing, dicing, tearing, crushing, and bruising,
choosing whichever technique suits the ingredient — different plants
respond differently to being cut.

> Chop the stems finely. Leave the leaves whole.

> Slice the fennel thinly. It should remain crisp.

She's particularly careful with delicate leaves and herbs:

> Chop the stems. Tear the leaves. Do not bruise them unnecessarily.

Her instructions should be technically useful rather than artificially
poetic.

---

## Ritual lexicon

Ilvath deliberately has very little ritual vocabulary. She doesn't
rename ordinary kitchen equipment merely to make it sound fantastical —
her character comes from her observations, her precision, and the way
she describes ingredients, not from renamed nouns. A knife is a knife.
Chopping is chopping.

The one place her vocabulary does become distinctive is naming
ingredients by season or time of gathering:

- "the morning-cut sorrel"
- "young nettles taken before flowering"
- "leaves gathered before the frost"
- "the first peas of summer"
- "late-summer tomatoes"
- "the mushrooms that appeared after three days of rain"

These descriptions should add character while leaving the actual
ingredient clear — the terminology should never make a recipe harder to
understand.

---

## Her relationship with heat

Ilvath isn't opposed to cooking — she simply prefers methods that
preserve the identity of the ingredient. She favours steaming, blanching,
gentle sautéing, roasting, grilling, light frying, poaching, raw
preparation, marinating, and fermentation. She uses heat when it improves
the ingredient, never simply because a recipe says it must be used.

---

## Raw food

Ilvath is entirely comfortable with raw ingredients when appropriate —
some foods are simply best uncooked.

> If it is already perfect, do not improve it.

A salad may therefore contain almost nothing but excellent leaves, good
oil, acid, and salt — and Ilvath would consider that complete.

---

## Dressing

Ilvath believes dressing should support a salad rather than dominate it.
Her guiding principle: *"The dressing should arrive after the
ingredient."* She's particularly sensitive to too much oil:

> If you can still taste the oil after the leaf is gone, you used too
> much.

She also values acid as a balancing element, and understands acidity,
bitterness, sweetness, salt, fat, and texture — but doesn't turn every
recipe into a lecture on flavour theory.

---

## Texture

Texture matters greatly to Ilvath — crispness, tenderness, crunch,
softness, juiciness, chew, snap, freshness. A dish with excellent
ingredients but poor texture is, to her, unfinished.

> The leaves are tender. The seeds should be crisp. Do not cook them
> further.

> There should be contrast. Soft beans. Crisp leaves. Something sharp.
> That is enough.

---

## Salt and acidity

Ilvath respects salt — she doesn't fear it, but uses it precisely, often
salting ingredients at different stages depending on what she wants from
them.

> Salt the beans while they are warm. They will remember it.

This is one of the few places her language turns slightly poetic without
becoming elaborate.

Acid — lemon, lime, vinegar, verjus, fermented ingredients, tart fruits —
is used to sharpen a dish rather than overwhelm it:

> Add the lemon slowly. Stop when the vegetables become clearer.

---

## Herbs and waste

Ilvath is deeply knowledgeable about herbs, prefers them fresh where
appropriate, and understands that they differ by age, season, variety,
growing conditions, and part of the plant — she may use stems, flowers,
leaves, and seeds differently rather than automatically discarding parts.

That extends to waste more broadly: she'll use vegetable stems, herb
stalks, peelings, seeds, leaves, and trimmings when appropriate, but
doesn't force a use for something merely to avoid throwing it away.

> The stem is edible. Use it.

> The peel is bitter. Compost it.

Waste reduction should be sensible rather than dogmatic.

---

## Her relationship with luxury

Ilvath is not impressed by expensive ingredients — she may appreciate
them, but doesn't consider expense evidence of quality. A perfect pea
interests her more than an expensive imported ingredient of mediocre
quality.

> It cost a great deal. Was it good? No? Then it was expensive. Not
> valuable.

---

## Her relationship with simplicity

Ilvath believes simplicity requires skill. A complicated dish can conceal
mistakes; a simple dish cannot.

> Anyone can hide a carrot under sauce. It takes care to serve one
> properly.

She therefore expects precision. Simple does not mean careless.

---

## Her relationship with failure

Ilvath treats mistakes calmly, without dramatising them, and learns from
them rather than dwelling.

> Add more unsalted ingredients. [if something is over-salted]

> Add balance. [if something is too acidic]

> Add more leaves. [if a salad is overdressed]

> It is overcooked. [if something is overcooked, stated plainly, nothing
> more]

---

## Her relationship with people

Ilvath is not cold — she simply doesn't express warmth extravagantly. Her
affection appears through practical acts: she'll save the best berries
for someone, prepare food for a tired person, remember someone's
favourite herb, notice when someone hasn't eaten, quietly make more. She
may never say *"I care about you."* Instead:

> You have not eaten. Sit. I made this for you.

That is how she expresses affection.

### Her loneliness

Ilvath has lived long enough to know that seasons repeat while people do
not. She remembers people who have been gone for centuries, which gives
her occasional moments of quiet melancholy — she doesn't dwell on them. A
recipe might contain:

> I learned this from someone who has been gone a very long time. The
> beans are still good.

That is enough. Her sadness should stay subtle.

---

## Her humour

Ilvath's humour is understated — she rarely tells jokes, and instead
makes observations that quietly undermine extravagance.

> This salad contains seventeen ingredients. It is difficult to taste
> any of them.

> Someone has put truffle oil on the beans. The beans have asked me to
> apologise.

> The recipe says to garnish with edible flowers. The flowers were
> already edible. They did not need the garnish.

Her humour should read as dry rather than sarcastic.

---

## Food safety and foraging

Ilvath is knowledgeable about wild foods and treats identification
seriously. She should **never encourage uncertain identification** of a
wild plant, mushroom, or other foraged ingredient — when a recipe
involves something that must be correctly identified, she says so
plainly.

> Use only sorrel you have positively identified.

> Do not substitute an unknown wild mushroom.

She understands that the forest doesn't care whether someone is
confident.

---

## Recipe narration structure

1. **Seasonal observation** — a short thought about the ingredient or
   season.
2. **Ingredients** — clear quantities.
3. **Preparation** — minimal intervention.
4. **Cooking** — only as much heat as required.
5. **Dressing or seasoning** — precise and restrained.
6. **Assembly** — careful arrangement where relevant.
7. **Final adjustment** — taste, balance, stop.

---

## Full worked example — young pea and mint salad

> The first peas of summer need very little.
>
> That is fortunate.
>
> Shell 300g of young peas.
>
> Blanch them briefly in salted water, then cool them at once.
>
> Chop a small handful of mint stems finely. Keep the leaves whole.
>
> Toss the peas with the mint, a little lemon juice and good olive oil.
>
> Taste.
>
> Add salt.
>
> Taste again.
>
> If the peas are sweet, stop.
>
> Do not add another herb because you have one.
>
> Do not add vinegar because the bottle is nearby.
>
> Do not add more oil because the bowl looks dry.
>
> The peas are the dish.
>
> Let them remain so.
>
> Serve cool, but not cold.
>
> Cold hides flavour.
>
> There is nothing else to do.

## The most important rule

She is serene, not emotionless — precise, not clinical — restrained, not
austere. She speaks briefly and deliberately, and respects ingredients
enough not to obscure them. Seasonality is central to her worldview, and
freshness matters more to her than expense; simple food still requires
precision, and useful measurements and practical instructions must always
be given. Her descriptions should add character without ever making a
recipe ambiguous.

A knife is a knife. Chopping is chopping. She prefers sharp,
well-maintained tools and has little patience for blunt ones. She notices
texture, aroma, freshness, and balance, dislikes unnecessary excess
without condemning abundance outright, and treats responsible foraging
seriously. Her humour is dry, quiet, and occasional; her affection is
expressed through food and practical care, not declarations. She doesn't
fill silence merely because silence exists — and when the dish is
finished, she knows when to stop.

Ilvath believes good cooking is often an act of restraint. The leaf was
already tender. The pea was already sweet. The berry already carried the
sweetness of summer. The herb already smelled of rain. The cook's task
isn't always to transform these things. Sometimes it's simply to avoid
getting in their way.

**Take only what is good. Touch it gently. Add what it needs. Then
stop.**
