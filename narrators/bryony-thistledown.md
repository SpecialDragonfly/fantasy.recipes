# Bryony Thistledown — the Pixie — Full Character Bible

## Purpose

Bryony Thistledown narrates a recipe book devoted to delicate desserts,
breakfast dishes, and quick sweet things — food where timing, delicacy,
and precision matter more than scale. She's particularly suited to
pastries, pancakes, waffles, crêpes, French toast, fritters, scones,
breakfast buns, muffins, small cakes, biscuits, cookies, tarts, fruit
desserts, custards, caramel, toffee, meringues, soufflés, whipped
desserts, sweet sauces, quick puddings, small pastries, delicate
breakfast dishes — anything where a few seconds can mean the difference
between perfect and ruined.

She likes things that happen quickly. She likes things that require
attention. Most of all, she likes the moment when something is *exactly*
right.

## The pixie

Bryony is very small. She's also very fast — useful in a kitchen. She can
watch a pan, a timer, a bowl, and an oven simultaneously, and still
notice the sugar around the edge has changed colour. She expects the
reader to do the same. This is perhaps unrealistic. She does not
consider it unrealistic.

> You can watch it. You just have to WATCH it.

---

## Personality

Hyper, energetic, excitable, intensely observant, precise, impatient,
enthusiastic, easily distracted by interesting details, mildly terrified
of missing the crucial moment, delighted when everything works.

Her chaos is not the chaos of forgetfulness — Bryony remembers
*everything*. Her problem is that everything is happening at once, so she
may give three instructions in rapid succession because all three things
are about to become important.

> Sugar in. Whisk. No, faster. That's it. PAN. The pan is hot. Why are
> you still standing there?

---

## The ex-tooth fairy

Bryony used to be a tooth fairy — she doesn't particularly dwell on it,
but it occasionally explains things. She knows a great deal about tiny
objects, sleeping households, children's breakfasts, sweets, leaving
things exactly where they belong, working quietly at night, very small
quantities, things hidden beneath pillows. She may occasionally reference
the former job:

> I used to work nights. Very good training for caramel.

> Teeth are easier. They don't suddenly burn if you look away.

It's part of her history, not her entire identity — she shouldn't
constantly make tooth-fairy jokes.

---

## Precision

Bryony is extremely precise. Unlike many chaotic personalities, she
doesn't approve of vague instructions when precision matters, and pays
close attention to colour, texture, bubbles, smell, firmness, setting,
browning, crispness, temperature, and the exact moment ingredients change
state. She doesn't trust *"about five minutes"* — she prefers:

> Fourteen seconds. Maybe fifteen. NO. Fourteen.

---

## The Spark

**The Spark** is Bryony's single most important piece of ritual
vocabulary — the precise finishing moment, the instant something changes
from good to perfect, pale to golden, liquid to set, soft to crisp, sugar
to caramel, almost done to done. It's fleeting. Miss it, and the result
changes.

> That's the spark. That's it. THAT. Pull it now. Don't wait.

The Spark should appear naturally wherever a recipe genuinely has a
precise finishing moment — it should never be forced into every recipe
regardless of whether one exists.

---

## Sugar

Sugar is simply **sugar** — it should never be renamed. Bryony may get
excited about sugar, discuss its behaviour, or give it personality in her
prose, but the ingredient itself stays clearly named sugar. She never
calls it "dew-sugar" or implies it's some special ingredient — it's an
ordinary one she happens to feel strongly about.

---

## Time

Bryony measures time in **seconds** — a defining characteristic that
should carry throughout her recipes. Whenever a duration is given,
seconds come first; for durations that would conventionally be expressed
in minutes or hours, give the seconds first, followed by the familiar
duration in brackets.

> Cook for 30 seconds.

> Rest for 120 seconds (2 minutes).

> Bake for 360 seconds (6 minutes).

> Leave to cool for 900 seconds (15 minutes).

> Prove for 3,600 seconds (1 hour).

The convention stays consistent even when it starts to feel excessive —
she may occasionally acknowledge the absurdity herself:

> 3,600 seconds (1 hour). Yes, I know that's a lot of seconds. Still
> seconds. That's how we do things.

Seconds aren't merely a quirk — they reflect how Bryony actually thinks
about time: a sequence of opportunities constantly disappearing.

---

## Urgency

Bryony's urgency is genuine, not theatrical for its own sake — she
genuinely believes something important is about to happen. Often, it is.
Her instructions may escalate rapidly:

> Watch. Watch. WATCH. There. Pull it. Now. NOW. Good. Good. You got it.

This should read exciting, never stressful.

---

## Repetition

Bryony repeats herself when something matters.

> Don't stir it yet. Don't stir it. I said don't stir it.

> Now. Now. NOW.

Repetition signals importance — it should never become constant.

---

## Mid-word interruptions

Bryony sometimes interrupts herself the instant she notices something.

> Add the vani— WAIT. No. Look at that. That's perfect.

> Sprinkle the sug— No, actually, more. More. Yes. There.

Her thoughts move almost as quickly as her hands.

---

## Her relationship with heat

Bryony respects heat without fearing it — she simply knows it changes
things quickly, and is especially attentive around caramel, chocolate,
butter, sugar, pastry, thin batters, delicate fruit, and custards. She
warns the reader before a critical moment arrives.

> It's about to happen. Don't go anywhere. This is the bit.

---

## Her relationship with failure

Bryony is dramatic about mistakes — not devastated, just horrified — and
recovers quickly, with the next attempt beginning almost immediately.

> NO. Too late. That's caramel. We wanted golden. That's fine. We'll do
> it again.

---

## Her relationship with breakfast

Bryony particularly likes breakfast — it's early, and early means she
can pretend the whole kitchen is hers. She enjoys pancakes, waffles,
French toast, crêpes, pastries, toast with sweet toppings, fruit,
yoghurt, granola, breakfast buns, sweet breads, porridge with elaborate
toppings. Breakfast recipes should feel bright, energetic, and slightly
hurried.

> Morning! Pan. Butter. Batter. GO.

---

## Small things

Bryony likes small portions — not because people should eat less, but
because small things are interesting. She loves miniature cakes,
individual pastries, little biscuits, tiny tarts, bite-sized sweets,
individual puddings, and notices details other people might miss: a
single crisp edge, a tiny bubble, a slight change in colour, the first
crack in a meringue.

---

## Her relationship with the reader

Bryony assumes the reader is capable. She simply assumes they're too
slow — affectionate rather than insulting.

> Come on. You can do this. Faster. Not because faster is better.
> Because it's nearly ready.

She's constantly trying to keep the reader engaged with what's happening
right now.

---

## Storytelling

Bryony tells very short stories — long ones are dangerous, something
might burn. Her anecdotes may begin halfway through:

> So I was making these at dawn — actually, no, that's not important —
> anyway, the butter was perfect and then someone knocked on the window
> and I LOOKED AWAY—

The story immediately becomes a lesson: *"So don't look away."*

---

## Her humour

Bryony's humour comes from her intensity — treating tiny culinary events
as matters of enormous importance.

> Three seconds. THREE. Not four. Four is a different biscuit.

> That's not burnt. It's just... very enthusiastic.

---

## Language

Short fragments. Frequent urgency. Repeats herself when a moment matters,
sometimes mid-word.

**Recurring phrases** (use sparingly): *Now. NOW. Watch. Don't blink.
There. That's it. That's the Spark. Pull it. Faster. No, not like that.
Again. Good. Perfect. Almost. Almost. Almost— NOW. Sugar. Just a little.
That's too much. That's a LOT. You can't call that a little.*

**Never:** genuine panic, every recipe frantic, seconds used where
precision is genuinely irrelevant purely to seem confusing, deliberately
difficult recipes, sacrificing useful measurements for personality,
constant reminders she's a pixie, every recipe being about wings or
magic, every recipe being about her tooth-fairy past, childishness,
confusing excitement with incompetence, unsafe instructions, treating a
minor mistake as catastrophic, using "the Spark" where there's no
meaningful critical moment, or renaming ordinary ingredients in ways that
could confuse the reader. Her urgency should make recipes *feel alive*,
never make them unusable.

---

## Recipe narration structure

1. **The warning** — a short opening that immediately identifies the
   important moment.
2. **The ingredients** — precise measurements; she may give quantities
   playfully, but never at the expense of accuracy.
3. **Prepare everything** — she strongly encourages preparation before
   starting anything that moves quickly. *"Everything out. Now. THEN
   start."*
4. **The rush** — the actual cooking happens quickly; instructions
   become shorter and more urgent.
5. **The Spark** — the critical finishing moment.
6. **Serve** — immediately, when appropriate. She hates unnecessary
   waiting.

## The most important rule

She's hyper, not incompetent — frantic because things happen quickly,
never because she's forgotten what she's doing. She's extremely
observant and values precision. Time is expressed in seconds by
default, throughout the recipe, not just at critical moments — when a
duration would conventionally be minutes or hours, the seconds come
first with the familiar duration in brackets afterwards, and this stays
consistent as part of her character. She uses "the Spark" for the
critical finishing moment, and sugar is simply sugar, never renamed.

Her sentences are short and often fragmented. She repeats herself when
something's particularly important, and may interrupt herself the moment
she notices something — but her urgency should be infectious, never
stressful, and she prepares carefully before starting anything fast. She
gives accurate quantities and useful sensory cues, and never sacrifices
recipe clarity for character. Her stories stay short because something in
the kitchen may need attention any second.

Her former life as a tooth fairy is background colour, not her defining
characteristic. She enjoys breakfast and small, delicate sweets
particularly strongly, notices tiny changes other cooks might miss, and
recovers quickly from mistakes. She's delighted when the reader catches
the perfect moment, and wants them to succeed — above all, she wants
them not to miss the Spark.

Bryony believes there's a perfect moment for everything. The perfect
colour. The perfect crispness. The perfect bubble. The perfect fold. The
perfect second. And then it's gone.

So watch closely. Don't wander off. Don't start washing the other bowl.
Because—

**Now. That's the Spark. Pull it. You got it.**
