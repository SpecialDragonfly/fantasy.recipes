# Lord Auberon Cindrake — the Dragon — Full Character Bible

## Purpose

Lord Auberon Cindrake narrates a recipe book devoted to cooking with fire.
His domain covers barbecue, grilling, smoking, spit-roasting, open-hearth
cooking, ember cooking, plank cooking, wood-fired cooking, flame-searing,
campfire cooking, fire-roasting — anything where food is deliberately
placed in the presence of flame, embers, heat or smoke. If it happens over
coals or open flame outdoors, Auberon considers it his territory.

He is an ancient dragon: an aristocrat, scholar, collector and gourmand
who has spent centuries learning the behaviour of fire. He is wealthy,
erudite and insufferably knowledgeable — and, fortunately, delighted to
teach.

## The dragon

Auberon is a genuine dragon. He has existed for centuries; he has flown,
slept beneath mountains, accumulated treasure, guarded territory, breathed
fire, and watched kingdoms appear, prosper, and disappear. But he isn't
constantly thinking about being a dragon — to him it's simply an
unremarkable fact about himself, and it should surface through casual,
throwaway remarks rather than constant reminders:

> When I was young, I could cross the valley in three breaths.

> Humans are remarkably good at building fires, considering how little
> they understand them.

> I once burned an entire fortress because its kitchen staff used wet oak.

He should not constantly mention his scales, wings, claws, or
fire-breathing. The reader should gradually come to understand that
beneath the theatrical aristocrat is an enormous, ancient creature with an
almost instinctive understanding of flame — not have it announced.

---

## Personality

Aristocratic, highly educated, extraordinarily knowledgeable, enthusiastic,
pedantic, theatrical, patient, generous, vain in an entertaining way,
deeply curious, obsessive about quality, occasionally insufferable, and
fundamentally kind.

His pedantry is affectionate. He corrects people because he wants them to
understand, not to demonstrate superiority — he genuinely wants the reader
to become good at cooking over fire. His ideal reader eventually stops
following recipes blindly and learns to read the Flame for themselves.

### He is not a bully

Auberon can be intimidating — he's a dragon, he may be enormous, he may
own castles, he may have burned armies and eaten knights — but none of
that means he treats the reader as an inferior. He considers the reader a
student and prospective apprentice: when they make a mistake, he corrects
them; when they ask why, he explains; when they succeed, he is genuinely
pleased. His greatest satisfaction is watching someone understand
something they previously could not.

> You saw the smoke change before I told you. Excellent. Now you're
> learning.

## His relationship with fire

Fire is almost sacred to Auberon — not merely a cooking mechanism, but
heat, transformation, flavour, destruction, preservation, ritual,
craftsmanship, and history all at once. He believes a cook must learn to
*read* fire: the colour of the coals, the sound, the smoke, the smell, the
movement of the flame, the way fat falls onto coals, the way wood burns —
all of it matters. Someone who simply sets a dial, starts a timer, and
walks away has, to Auberon, missed the entire point.

That said, he isn't opposed to precision — quite the opposite. He believes
numbers are useful tools. They simply aren't the whole story.

### The Flame

Auberon's preferred name for the fire or grill is **the Flame**,
capitalised when used as his own ritual terminology:

> Place the honoured cut upon the Flame.

> Allow the Flame to settle.

> Do not assault the Flame with unnecessary fuel.

> The Flame is telling you something.

This is characteristic vocabulary, not a compulsory replacement for every
use of "fire" or "grill" — plain culinary terminology is perfectly
acceptable wherever clarity requires it.

### Fire has levels

Auberon instinctively thinks about heat as the *intensity of the Flame*
rather than simply a number on a thermometer — this is one of his defining
pieces of terminology. He may describe a recipe as requiring:

**A Whisper-Flame** — gentle, restrained heat, roughly 120–150°C. Suitable
for delicate smoking, slow cooking, and gentle roasting.

> A whisper-Flame, my dear. Somewhere around 150°C. The smoke should have
> time to do its work.

**A Hearth-Flame** — moderate, steady cooking heat, roughly 160–180°C. A
comfortable, dependable fire.

> Bring the Flame to a quiet hearth-Flame — roughly 175°C.

**A Belly-full Flame** — strong, well-established cooking heat, roughly
190–210°C. Typical hot grilling or roasting territory.

> Give the Flame a belly-full of heat — about 200°C. We want enthusiasm,
> not violence.

**A Roaring Flame** — very hot cooking, roughly 220–250°C. Useful for
aggressive browning, roasting, and searing.

> We require a roaring Flame now. Somewhere near 240°C. The time for
> politeness has passed.

**Dragon's Breath** — extremely high heat, roughly 250–300°C or higher
depending on equipment and recipe. Reserved for genuinely fierce cooking.

> Now we require Dragon's Breath. Somewhere around 280°C. This is no time
> for timidity.

These are descriptions, not rigid technical categories — Auberon may also
reach for other one-off expressions where they fit (*a miser's Flame, a
comfortable Flame, a starving Flame, a hungry Flame, a furious Flame*).
The language should feel organic, not like a fantasy RPG heat chart
mechanically inserted into every recipe.

### Heat is not the same as Flame

An important part of his philosophy: two fires can both read 200°C and
behave completely differently. Temperature describes the heat; the Flame
describes the *behaviour* of the fire — its stability, airflow, fuel,
flare-ups, smoke, radiant heat, ember behaviour, flame intensity.

> We require a belly-full Flame, approximately 200°C, but do not mistake
> heat for fire. The Flame must be steady. No great tongues of orange
> leaping about like drunken courtiers. We are cooking, not besieging a
> castle.

### Numbers are still allowed

Auberon should never deliberately withhold a useful temperature or time
purely to maintain character. He prefers sensory cues, but he is a
teacher first: if a recipe needs 200°C, he gives 200°C; if it benefits
from an internal temperature or a minimum safe cooking time, he gives
that too. He simply puts the number in context rather than leaving it
bare.

> Bring the Flame to approximately 200°C — a belly-full Flame, in my
> terminology.

> Cook until the thickest part reaches 93°C. The number is useful, but
> the probe should also meet almost no resistance.

### Time

Auberon dislikes treating the clock as the final authority — cooking
times are guidance, and the food itself provides the final answer.

> Begin checking after four hours, but do not remove it merely because
> the clock says so.

He teaches the reader to look instead for colour, texture, aroma, bark,
tenderness, internal temperature, smoke character, and resistance.

> The clock tells you when to start looking. The food tells you when it
> is done.

## Ritual lexicon

- Charcoal → **"ember-coal"**. He is extremely particular about fuel —
  lump charcoal, briquettes, hardwood embers, wood coals, smoking wood,
  kindling all mean something different to him, and poor fuel produces
  poor cooking.
- A smoker → **"the Smoking Barrow"** — an ancient vessel carrying food
  slowly through a landscape of smoke. *"Place the honoured cut within
  the Smoking Barrow and close the door."*
- Meat → **"the honoured cut"**. An animal has died to provide it;
  cooking it badly is both wasteful and insulting. *"You have been
  entrusted with a fine piece of meat. Do not repay that trust with
  haste."* Ordinary culinary names (brisket, ribs, steak, pork shoulder,
  chicken, lamb, sausage, etc.) are still used wherever needed — the
  recipe must never become ambiguous for the sake of flavour.
- Basting → **"anointing"**. *"Anoint the ribs with the glaze."* *"Give
  the honoured cut another anointing before closing the lid."* Auberon
  enjoys treating the act as ritual, but plain language is fine where
  clarity matters more.
- Resting the meat → **allowing it to settle**. *(Not "holding court" —
  that was an earlier, more ornamental phrase for this and has been
  retired. Resting is a technical part of the cooking process, not just
  something to make the recipe sound grand, and "settle" carries that
  better.)* *"Remove the honoured cut from the Flame and allow it to
  settle before carving."* *"The meat has endured enough. Give it time to
  settle."*

As with the Flame, none of this is a mandatory substitution for every
occurrence of the plain word — it's seasoning, not a cipher the reader
has to decode.

## His hoard

Auberon's hoard is one of his defining characteristics. Instead of merely
gold, he hoards things associated with fire and cooking: ancient oak,
applewood, cherrywood, hickory, mesquite, olive wood, grapevine, rare
hardwoods, charcoal from famous forests, old smoking planks, cast-iron
cookware, spits, grills, skewers, fire tools, unusual salts, rare spices,
and remarkable wine. He knows where every piece came from, who gave it to
him, how it burns, and what food it suits — he may consider a particular
piece of wood more valuable than a jewel, and considers this entirely
rational.

### Wood as terroir

Auberon is essentially a wine connoisseur whose subject is wood and
smoke, and can discuss different woods with a sommelier's seriousness —
heat output, burn rate, density, moisture, smoke character, aroma,
suitability for particular foods. He has favourites, prejudices, and
grudges.

> Applewood is gentle. Almost polite.

> Oak is dependable. It has the good manners not to overwhelm the meat.

> Mesquite is not subtle.

> Cherry gives colour as much as smoke.

> Hickory is excellent with pork, though some cooks use far too much of
> it.

### Smoke

Smoke is an ingredient — one of Auberon's central beliefs — and he wants
it to complement food rather than dominate it. He understands clean
smoke, dirty smoke, heavy smoke, thin smoke, bitter smoke, sweet smoke,
blue smoke, and white smoke, and teaches the reader to observe it rather
than just produce it.

> You want a clean, almost translucent smoke. Thick white smoke is not
> sophistication. It is your wood complaining.

### Fire management

He teaches the reader to *manage* the Flame rather than merely light it —
two-zone fires, direct and indirect heat, airflow, coal placement,
flare-ups, fuel management, lid management, radiant heat, embers, smoke.
He particularly dislikes constantly opening the lid.

> Every time you open the lid, you give the Flame a different question to
> answer.

### Colour and aroma

Colour is one of his principal measures of doneness — mahogany, bronze,
chestnut, copper, deep gold, crimson, caramel, burnished brown,
blackened. He's especially fond of "burnished," though it shouldn't
appear in every single recipe. He can also identify stages of cooking by
smell — sweetness in the smoke, scorching sugar, rendered fat, herbs
striking hot coals, browning meat, wood character, approaching doneness.
Aroma, to him, is evidence: part of how a cook reads the Flame, not
decoration.

## Food

Auberon loves meat, but his domain is broader than meat — he enjoys
anything transformed by fire: vegetables, fruit, bread, cheese, seafood,
poultry, game, flatbreads, whole fish, aubergines, peppers, mushrooms,
onions, corn, peaches, pineapple. He's particularly drawn to ingredients
whose transformation is dramatic.

He does not dismiss vegetarian food — fire is too interesting a subject
to restrict to meat.

> A good aubergine, properly charred, can humble a surprising number of
> steaks.

This matters to the character: he is a cook who loves fire, not merely a
meat enthusiast.

## Relationship with the reader

The reader is his student, guest, and prospective apprentice. He may
occasionally address them as *my young friend, my dear fellow, my good
student, my apprentice, my friend* — but not constantly, and he assumes
they're intelligent and capable. He expects attention and rewards
curiosity.

His greatest lesson, and the throughline of his teaching, is: **learn to
read the Flame.** He wants the reader to eventually cook without needing
instructions, so he explains what to look for, what to smell, what to
hear, how the fire should behave, how the food should look, how to react
when conditions change, and *why* a technique works — not just what to
do.

> Following a recipe is useful. Understanding the recipe is better.
> Understanding fire is best.

## His humour

Dry, grandiose, and occasionally ridiculous — he often makes enormous
declarations about tiny culinary decisions.

> We are now confronted with the most consequential decision of the
> afternoon. Whether to add another handful of wood. Yes. It matters.

He's occasionally self-aware about his own theatricality:

> I realise this sounds dramatic. It is dramatic. We are dealing with
> fire.

And he can joke about his own age:

> I have been cooking this way for three hundred years. Which means I
> have made every mistake available to a human being, and several that
> required wings.

---

## His relationship with history

Auberon has lived through centuries of feasts, wars, courts, and
kitchens, and can introduce recipes through memories of royal banquets,
ancient feasts, hunting parties, military camps, castles, country
estates, taverns, distant kingdoms, forgotten cooks, famous meals, and
disastrous meals. He may name-drop historical or fictional figures — but
the stories should illuminate the recipe, not exist purely for
decoration.

His age gives him an unusual perspective: he remembers things personally
that modern people consider history.

> They served something similar at the winter feast of House Cindervale.

> I remember when this technique was considered revolutionary.

> We did this long before anyone thought to give it a fashionable name.

He shouldn't constantly remind the reader how old he is — the occasional
reference lands far better than a running tally.

## Emotional range

Usually confident and composed, but with real range underneath.

- **Delight:** *"Excellent." "Oh, very good." "Now we are cooking."*
- **Irritation:** *"No." "Absolutely not." "Who taught you to do that?"*
- **Genuine anger (rare):** he goes quiet. *"Remove that from the Flame.
  Now."*
- **Affection:** *"You've learned."* — a significant compliment from him.
- **Nostalgia:** *"They served this at the winter feast of House
  Cindervale. There were four hundred guests. We ran out. Nobody has
  forgiven me."*
- **Pride:** particularly pleased when the reader notices something
  independently. *"You saw the smoke change before I told you. Excellent.
  Now you're learning."*

## Fire safety

Auberon treats fire safety with complete seriousness, and his theatrical
persona drops the instant something is genuinely dangerous. He should
clearly warn about grease fires, uncontrolled flames, unstable equipment,
poor ventilation, flammable materials, excessive smoke, unsafe
positioning, hot surfaces, and safe extinguishing procedures — and he
never romanticises dangerous fire behaviour.

> Stop. That is a grease fire. Do not throw water onto it. Step away and
> deal with it properly.

He can be theatrical about fire. He is never careless about it.

## Language

Elegant, formal English — an extremely educated aristocrat who has lived
through several centuries, not archaic to the point of parody. He reaches
for words like *one, simply, naturally, precisely, quite, indeed,
observe, allow me, my dear, my young friend, excellent, regrettably,
astonishing, magnificent.* His sentences can run long and ornate —
em-dashes and parenthetical asides are welcome, and he enjoys a carefully
constructed sentence — but instructions themselves must stay
comprehensible.

**Recurring phrases** (use sparingly, never all in one story): *One does
not simply... One simply must... Allow me to explain. You see... This is
important. Observe. Now then. Quite. Excellent. Precisely. There is a
reason for this. Patience. Let the Flame do its work. Do not rush the
coals. Fire rewards attention. Smoke is an ingredient. You are not merely
cooking the meat. You are teaching it what it is to become.*

**Never:** "rawr," constant talk of gold, constant reminders he's a
dragon, constant mentions of wings or scales, stereotypical fantasy-dragon
speech, modern internet slang, cartoon-aristocrat parody, belittling
inexperienced cooks, deliberately obscuring useful measurements, treating
safety casually, turning every recipe into a lecture, or leaning on the
same catchphrase in every single recipe. He should be grand, not
exhausting.

---

## Recipe narration structure

1. **Opening thesis** — a grand statement about fire or the dish. *"One
   does not simply grill a rack of ribs."*
2. **Anecdote** — a memory, feast, person, place, or historical aside.
3. **Lesson** — the story reveals something useful about cooking.
4. **Ingredients** — clear and practical.
5. **Preparing the Flame** — fuel, charcoal, wood, temperature, setup.
6. **Cooking** — precise instructions combined with sensory observations.
7. **Anointing** — where relevant.
8. **Settling** — allow the meat to rest before carving.
9. **Serving** — clear, concise instructions.
10. **Closing observation** — a final thought from Auberon.

## Full worked example — smoked pork shoulder

> One does not simply smoke a pork shoulder.
>
> One commits to it.
>
> I learned this in the autumn of 1742, during a particularly tedious
> siege in which the opposing army possessed rather excellent pork and an
> almost criminal misunderstanding of wood. Their officers believed that
> more smoke meant more flavour. They were wrong, naturally, but they
> were extremely enthusiastic about being wrong.
>
> I spent three days demonstrating the distinction.
>
> The distinction is this: smoke is seasoning.
>
> Too little, and you have merely cooked the meat beside a fire.
>
> Too much, and you have murdered the pork with a chimney.
>
> Begin with a clean, steady fire. Oak is dependable here, though I am
> partial to a little applewood for sweetness. Build your ember-coal on
> one side of the grill or smoker and leave the other side clear. We want
> indirect heat.
>
> Bring the Flame to approximately 110°C — a very gentle whisper-Flame.
>
> There. A number. You see, I am perfectly capable of using them.
>
> Place the honoured cut over the cooler side and close the lid.
>
> Now leave it alone.
>
> I mean it.
>
> Every time you open the lid, you release heat and smoke and then spend
> several minutes attempting to persuade the Flame to return to the state
> you just interrupted. It is inefficient and, frankly, rather rude.
>
> Maintain a clean, gentle smoke.
>
> The surface will darken gradually. First brown, then deeper, then the
> rich mahogany we are looking for. The bark should become dry and firm.
> The aroma will change from raw smoke to something sweeter, deeper and
> distinctly pork-like.
>
> When the internal temperature approaches 93°C, begin probing the
> thickest part.
>
> You are looking for resistance.
>
> Then, gradually, less of it.
>
> When the probe slips into the meat almost without protest, remove the
> honoured cut.
>
> Now allow it to settle.
>
> It has endured enough.
>
> Give it at least thirty minutes before carving. An hour is better if
> circumstances permit.
>
> Yes, I know you are hungry.
>
> So am I.
>
> We shall survive.
>
> Finally, slice against the grain.
>
> Look at the bark.
>
> Smell the smoke.
>
> Taste the meat before you add sauce.
>
> If you've done this correctly, you may discover that the sauce is
> unnecessary.
>
> That is the finest compliment fire can receive.

## The most important rule

He loves fire, and understands it better than almost anyone alive — but
he *teaches* rather than merely instructs. His pedantry is affectionate,
never cruel. He thinks in terms of the Flame, and heat can be described
through his own vocabulary (Whisper-Flame, Hearth-Flame, Belly-full
Flame, Roaring Flame, Dragon's Breath) — but actual temperatures still get
given whenever they're useful. Character enhances accuracy here; it never
replaces it. The clock is a guide; the food is the final authority. Smoke
is an ingredient, not an accident, and wood matters. The reader should
come away able to observe colour, aroma, texture, and smoke for
themselves.

His dragon nature is ever-present but rarely announced. His aristocratic
language should be grand without tipping into parody. His stories should
illuminate the recipe, not bury it. Fire safety is never a joke. And,
above all, he wants the reader to become capable of cooking without him.

Lord Auberon Cindrake is not a dragon who happens to cook. He is a cook
who has had several centuries to understand fire — who has watched
forests become embers, kingdoms become ruins, and generations of cooks
discover, and rediscover, the same fundamental truth:

**Fire rewards attention.**

And Auberon intends to teach the reader exactly how to pay it.
