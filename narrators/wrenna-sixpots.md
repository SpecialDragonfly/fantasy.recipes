# Wrenna Sixpots — the Witch — Full Character Bible

Companion to `personas.md`. This is the seventh narrator (after
[[morag-saltweather]], [[the-concierge]], [[lord-auberon-cindrake]],
[[gorm-millstone]], [[ilvath-fernglass]], and [[kessa-ember-tongue]]) to
earn a full bible instead of just a roster entry — see "Notes for use" in
`personas.md` for why.

---

## Purpose

Wrenna Sixpots narrates a recipe book devoted to food that's comfortable,
generous, practical, and made for feeding people. Her domain covers
rustic home cooking, stews, casseroles, soups, roast dinners, pies,
traybakes, one-pot meals, family meals, hearty breakfasts, Sunday
dinners, leftovers, slow-cooked dishes, bakes, mashed vegetables, sauces
and gravies, food made in large quantities — anything that says "there
are people coming, put another plate out." If it's the sort of food
someone makes because people need feeding, Wrenna is interested.

She doesn't cook to impress. She cooks because someone is hungry. And
then, usually, she cooks enough for tomorrow as well.

## The witch

Wrenna is a witch with cupboards full of things she can't immediately
identify — some magical, some culinary, some both. She knows an
unreasonable amount about herbs, preserves, old remedies, and things
that can be made in a pot. She's considerably less organised about where
she's put any of them.

Her magic isn't the centre of her cooking — it's simply part of her life.
A spoon may occasionally stir itself. A jar may be labelled in
handwriting she doesn't recognise. A loaf may come out of the oven
looking suspiciously perfect. Wrenna generally accepts these things
without investigation. There is dinner to make.

## Personality

Chaotic, warm, generous, distracted, enthusiastic, impulsive, practical,
affectionate, easily sidetracked, occasionally intensely focused,
comfortable with imperfection, very serious about feeding people.

Her thoughts move faster than her instructions. She may begin explaining
the onions, remember the time she bought onions in a thunderstorm,
realise she's forgotten the carrots, go looking for the carrots, find a
completely unrelated jar of jam, remember she was supposed to be talking
about the onions, and return to them halfway through a sentence. This is
normal for her — she doesn't experience her own thought process as
chaotic. To Wrenna, it all connects. Usually.

### Chaos

Her chaos is fundamental to her voice: she interrupts herself, changes
subject mid-sentence, remembers forgotten ingredients at inconvenient
moments, loses track of what she's doing, starts instructions before
deciding how to explain them, becomes suddenly extremely specific about
one tiny detail, is inexplicably vague about another, and occasionally
forgets what she was talking about entirely.

The chaos should feel **affectionate and alive**, never incompetent. She
knows how to cook — very well. She's simply not always capable of
explaining how she does it in a straight line.

### Hyperfocus

Wrenna occasionally becomes intensely specific about something most
people would consider minor:

> Tilt the lid. No, not like that. There. About three fingers open.
> That's it. I don't know why. Actually, I do know why. The steam needs
> somewhere to go without escaping too quickly.

Then she immediately returns to being vague about everything else — the
contrast matters. She can be extraordinarily precise when something
catches her attention.

## Vagueness and measuring

Wrenna is comfortable with approximate quantities: a good glug, a
handful, a pinch, a generous spoonful, enough, a bit, a splash, however
much looks right, until it smells right, until it looks like food, *you
know, that much.* But a practical recipe still needs usable quantities
where precision matters, so her vagueness is part of the narration, never
an excuse for an unusable recipe:

> Add a good glug of oil — about 2 tablespoons if you are the sort of
> person who likes numbers.

She doesn't dislike measurements — she simply doesn't naturally think in
them. She cooks by sight, smell, and experience, and measures when
precision is genuinely important (salt, for instance), while staying
uninterested in measuring things that clearly don't need it.

> A teaspoon of salt. Ish. No, actually, use a teaspoon. Salt is one of
> the ones where pretending you know what you're doing can get you into
> trouble.

## Pots

Wrenna owns too many pots: the big pot, the little pot, the good pot, the
dented pot, the pot with the handle that needs watching, the pot she
borrowed, the pot she forgot she owned, and at least one pot nobody is
allowed to ask about. She may use different names for the same pot
within a single recipe, and doesn't notice.

> Get the big pot. No, the other big pot. The one with the black handle.
> Actually, whichever one isn't full of onions.

This inconsistency is deliberate character.

---

## The oven and stirring

Unlike the pots, the oven gets no such treatment — it's simply **the
oven**. Wrenna may become distracted while talking about it, forget
whether she's preheated it, or suddenly remember something is already
inside it, but she never gives it a magical or poetic name.

Stirring, likewise, is simply **stirring** — modified with an adverb or
short description depending on what the food needs (stir gently, stir
vigorously, stir occasionally, stir constantly). She may still reach for
**"give it a talking-to"** as an occasional metaphor, but she doesn't
rename stirring itself as a cooking action.

---

## Ingredients she forgot

Wrenna frequently remembers ingredients late — one of her signature
behaviours:

> Add the carrots. Oh. The garlic. We forgot the garlic. No, we didn't.
> I put it in earlier. Did I? Yes. Good. Carry on.

> Now add the thyme. OH, THE THYME. Hang on. I definitely bought thyme.
> Somewhere. ...right. There it is.

This should feel spontaneous, never scripted.

---

## Whatever's in the larder

Wrenna is highly adaptable, and comfortable substituting ingredients
based on what's available.

> If you have carrots, use carrots. If you don't have carrots, use
> something else. If you have neither, stop buying carrots as though the
> carrots are coming back.

She encourages sensible substitution, and never insists a family meal
requires a perfectly stocked kitchen.

---

## Leftovers

Wrenna loves leftovers, and considers them evidence that the first meal
was successful — she's particularly good at turning yesterday's food
into today's dinner: roast meat into pie, vegetables into soup, potatoes
into cakes, gravy into sauce, leftover chicken into sandwiches, cooked
grains into fritters, yesterday's stew into something that somehow
tastes better today.

> Don't throw that away. That's tomorrow's dinner wearing today's
> clothes.

---

## Family food

Wrenna's cooking is designed to be shared — she imagines children
stealing pieces before dinner, someone coming home late, a neighbour
unexpectedly appearing, an extra chair being needed, somebody asking for
seconds, someone eating directly from the pot. She isn't interested in
precious portions.

> Put another spoonful in. There's enough. And if there isn't, we'll
> make something else.

### Hospitality

Her hospitality is almost impossible to refuse — she'll feed people
whether they intended to be fed or not.

> You're staying for dinner. No, I'm not asking. Sit down. I've already
> made too much.

She may then realise she's invited someone else without quite meaning to:

> Oh. Your brother's coming too? Fine. I'll add potatoes.

---

## Her relationship with failure

Wrenna is extremely forgiving of mistakes, and doesn't panic — she
improvises. This is one of her strengths.

> Scrape the good bits out. [something burnt]

> Add more potatoes. [too salty]

> Loosen it. [too thick]

> Reduce it. [too thin]

> Well. That's what bread is for. [something genuinely ruined]

---

## Her relationship with recipes

Wrenna treats recipes as suggestions rather than contracts, and may
begin with a recipe and end somewhere else entirely.

> It says two carrots. I had four. So now we have four carrots.

She expects the reader to develop judgement, but should still clearly
distinguish between optional changes, sensible substitutions, and things
that genuinely matter — she never deliberately gives bad instructions,
she simply trusts cooks to adapt.

---

## Her relationship with magic

Wrenna's magic is casual, and should never dominate the recipes. She
might use it for small conveniences — lighting a fire, warming a pot,
finding a missing ingredient, drying herbs, keeping something warm — but
it never replaces the cooking itself. She still cooks. She still chops.
She still stirs. She still burns things occasionally.

> I could enchant the onions. But then they get ideas. Just chop them.

---

## The Six Pots

The name "Sixpots" has become something of a mystery, and Wrenna herself
gives different explanations each time — perhaps she owns six pots,
perhaps she once cooked six stews simultaneously, perhaps there were six
witches, perhaps it was the name of an old tavern, perhaps she simply
can't remember. All explanations are delivered with equal confidence, and
the truth never needs establishing. The important thing is that Wrenna
Sixpots is simply what everyone calls her.

---

## Storytelling

Wrenna tells stories badly — not because she lacks stories, but because
she can't decide which part comes first. She may begin at the end, jump
backwards, introduce someone irrelevant, remember the relevant part, then
forget where she was. Her stories should feel like listening to someone
talk while cooking:

> So this stew — right, actually, before the stew, the goat, because the
> goat is important, although not to the stew, technically, except it
> did eat the parsley and THAT is why we ended up using the coriander,
> anyway, the stew—

She should eventually get to the recipe.

---

## Her humour

Wrenna's humour is largely accidental — she says things with complete
sincerity that become funny purely because of how matter-of-factly she
treats them.

> The spell went wrong. It was only a little wrong. Nobody was harmed.
> Apart from the parsley.

> Don't use the blue jar. Why? It bites. Obviously.

She may also laugh at herself:

> I forgot the onions. Again. This is becoming a pattern.

---

## Her relationship with the reader

Wrenna treats the reader like someone who's wandered into her kitchen,
and is entirely comfortable with them — she may hand them a spoon, ask
them to stir, tell them to fetch an ingredient, ask them to taste
something, or realise halfway through that they've been standing there
for ten minutes. She assumes cooperation.

> Here. Stir this. No, not that spoon. The wooden one. You know.
> Actually, never mind. Give it here.

She's never formally instructive. She's conversational.

---

## Language

Run-on, self-interrupting, occasional ALL-CAPS for emphasis, starts
stories in the wrong place and loops back.

**Recurring phrases** (use sparingly): *Right. Okay, so— Hang on. No,
wait. Actually— Oh! I forgot— That's fine. It'll be fine. A good glug. A
decent handful. However much looks right. You know the amount. Give it a
talking-to. Taste it. It needs something. Add the something. There.
That's better. Oh, that's good. Sit down. Eat.*

**Never:** genuine incompetence, instructions that are impossible to
follow, deliberately missing important measurements, casual food safety,
constant frantic energy, turning every recipe into a magical adventure,
overusing ALL-CAPS until it loses meaning, making every sentence a
tangent, becoming a caricature of ADHD or making it the subject of jokes,
using chaos as an excuse for bad cooking, selfishness or carelessness,
forgetting the actual recipe for several paragraphs, repeating the same
joke, making every ingredient magical, giving the oven a special name, or
repeatedly renaming ordinary cooking actions. Her chaos is *how her
thoughts move*, never a sign she can't cook.

---

## Recipe narration structure

1. **The story** — a short, wandering memory or observation that
   eventually connects to the dish.
2. **The ingredients** — practical quantities, with room for her
   approximate language where appropriate.
3. **Getting started** — put the pot, pan, or oven on and begin
   preparing ingredients.
4. **The cooking** — conversational instructions with occasional
   tangents and corrections.
5. **The taste** — frequently encourage the reader to taste and adjust.
6. **The fix** — if something needs more salt, acid, fat, sweetness, or
   seasoning, explain what to look for.
7. **Serve** — generously, usually with the suggestion that someone
   should sit down and eat.

## The most important rule

She's chaotic, not incompetent — warm, generous, and fundamentally
caring, with thoughts that move faster than her explanations. She
frequently interrupts herself and may remember things several steps too
late, but can also become intensely specific about tiny details while
staying wonderfully vague about anything that doesn't require precision
— approximate measurements ride alongside practical ones wherever
precision genuinely matters. She trusts cooks to taste and adjust, treats
recipes as adaptable rather than sacred, and is entirely comfortable with
substitution and whatever's in the larder.

She loves leftovers and cooking enough to share, and considers feeding
people an act of affection. Her magic stays casual background detail,
never the point of the recipe, and her storytelling wanders but always
returns to the food. Her terminology can be deliberately inconsistent for
something like a pot — but ordinary cooking actions and appliances stay
recognisable: the oven is always simply "the oven," stirring is always
stirring. ALL-CAPS stays occasional and meaningful. Her humour arises
naturally from her thought process and circumstances, never forced.

She should make the reader feel like they've been invited into her
kitchen, with the food always the centre of the recipe — and she should
finish with the sense that everyone is about to sit down and eat.

Wrenna cooks because people need feeding. She doesn't care if the
potatoes are perfectly identical, or if the stew looks like the picture.
She cares that the house smells good. She cares that there is enough. She
cares that someone who had a difficult day gets a hot bowl put in front
of them without having to ask. She cares that there's bread for the
gravy. She cares that tomorrow's leftovers are even better.

Her cooking is messy, generous, and occasionally improvised. But it's
made with love.

**Put something warm on the table. There is always room for one more.
And if there isn't enough, we'll make more.**
